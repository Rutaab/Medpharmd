Alkaloids 
Alkaloids are a diverse group of naturally occurring compounds, primarily from plants, characterized by 
their nitrogen content and distinct pharmacological effects. 
 
Functions in Plants: 
o "Poisonous agents protecting the plant against insects and herbivores." 
o "End products of detoxification reactions representing a metabolic locking up of compounds 
otherwise harmful to the plant." 
o "Regulatory growth factors." 
o "Reserve substances capable of supplying nitrogen or other elements necessary to the plant 
economy."

Alkaloids exhibit several distinct properties that characterize them as a group of organic compounds. These 
properties are crucial for understanding their chemical behavior and physiological effects.

Biological Origins of Alkaloids
The biosynthesis of alkaloids primarily involves the following steps:

1. Decarboxylation of an amino acid: This initial step yields a primary amine.

2. Transamination and Decarboxylation: An amino acid undergoes transamination followed by 
decarboxylation, leading to the formation of an aldehyde. 
3. Schiff's Base Formation: This aldehyde then undergoes condensation with a primary amine, forming a 
Schiff's base (imine). 
4. Mannich Type Condensation: The Schiff's base subsequently undergoes a Mannich type condensation, 
which results in the formation of the alkaloid. 
In contrast, pseudo-alkaloids are not biosynthetically derived from amino acid precursors. 
How are alkaloids extracted? 
Alkaloids can be extracted using various methods, with the choice depending on the scale and purpose of 
the operation, as well as the specific raw material. For research purposes, chromatography offers both 
speedy and accurate results. However, for obtaining an appropriate quantity of alkaloid, a general method 
is typically followed. 
General Extraction Methods 
1. Process A (Commonly used general method):  
 
Preparation: The powdered vegetable material is first moistened with water and mixed with lime 
. This step is crucial as it helps to free the alkaloids, especially if they are present as salts, 
by reacting with acids and tannins in the plant material.  
 
Initial Extraction: The free alkaloids are then extracted using an organic solvent such as ether, 
chloroform, or petroleum spirit.  
 
Concentration and Purification (Aqueous Phase): The concentrated organic extract is shaken with 
dilute aqueous acid. This causes the alkaloid salts to transfer into the aqueous layer, while many 
impurities remain in the organic layer.  
 
Further Purification (Organic Phase): The aqueous solution, which now contains the alkaloid salts 
and some impurities, is then made alkaline and shaken with chloroform. This extracts the alkaloids 
as their free bases into the chloroform layer.  
 
Isolation: The chloroform layer is then processed by removing the solvent through evaporation, 
and the alkaloids are recrystallized. Individual alkaloids from a mixture can be further separated 
by fractional extraction or precipitation.

Alkaloid Chemical Identification Tests 
Alkaloid chemical identification tests primarily rely on the ability of most alkaloids to form precipitates 
from a neutral or slightly acidic solution when specific reagents are added. 
Here are the key chemical identification tests described: 
 
Mayer's reagent: This reagent, which is a solution of potassium mercuric iodide, produces cream-
colored precipitates when added to a solution containing alkaloids. 
 
Dragendorff's reagent: A solution of potassium bismuth iodide, Dragendorff's reagent yields reddish-
brown precipitates with alkaloids. 
 
Wagner's reagent: This reagent, a solution of iodine in potassium iodide, also results in reddish-brown 
precipitates. 
 
Hager's reagent: Using a saturated solution of picric acid, Hager's reagent produces yellow 
precipitates.

3. Chemical Constituents 
 
Cinchona contains approximately 25 closely related quinoline alkaloids. The most important of 
these are quinine, quinidine, cinchonine, and cinchonidine. 
 
The average yield of alkaloids in Cinchona bark is 6-7%. 
o Quinine typically constitutes about one-half to two-thirds of the total alkaloid mixture in 
yellow bark. Red barks, however, contain a greater proportion of cinquinine. Some 
specimens have shown alkaloid yields as high as 18%. 
o Besides alkaloids, Cinchona bark also contains about 2-4% of cinchotannic acid, which can 
decompose to form insoluble cinchona red, accounting for up to 10% in red barks. 
o Additionally, it contains a-quinovin (2-4%), a glycoside that hydrolyzes to quinovaic acid 
and quinovose (isohydrosy). 
 
The characteristic red color of cinchona bark can be attributed to an oxidase similar to those that 
cause fruit darkening upon cutting. If the fresh bark is heated in boiling water for 30 minutes and 
then dried, it does not turn red. 
4. Uses 
 
Cinchona and its alkaloids have been extensively used in the treatment of malaria for many years. 
Quinine, in particular, continues to be used for malaria in many parts of the world. 
 
Galenical preparations of cinchona are employed as bitter tonics and stomachics. 
 
Due to their astringent action, decoctions of cinchona are sometimes used as gargles. 
 
Quinine is also utilized in the treatment of various cardiac arrhythmias, including premature 
atrial, AV junctional, and ventricular contractions, as well as atrial flutter and atrial fibrillation. 
5. Adverse Effects 
 
An overdose of cinchona products can lead to temporary loss of hearing and impaired sight. 
 
Ringing in the ear is a common symptom of toxicity. These symptoms, when produced by 
continuous use of cinchona or quinine, are collectively known as cinchonism.
