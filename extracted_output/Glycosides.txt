Glycosides 
I. Overview of Glycosides 
 
Glycosides are chemical compounds that, upon hydrolysis, yield one or more sugar molecules. The 
most common sugar component is β-D-glucose, though rhamnose, digitoxose, and other sugars 
can also be present.  
 
Chemically, glycosides are acetals where the hydroxyl group of the sugar condenses with a hydroxyl 
group of a non-sugar component to form an "oxide ring." The non-sugar part is known as the 
"aglycone," and the sugar part is called the "glycone." 
II. Properties of Glycosides 
 
Physical State: Colorless, solid, amorphous, nonvolatile. Some may be colored (e.g., flavonoid-
yellow, anthraquinone-red or orange). 
 
Chemical Reactions: Give a positive reaction with Molisch's and Fehling's solution tests after 
hydrolysis. 
 
Solubility: Water soluble compounds, insoluble in organic solvents. 
 
Taste: Most have a bitter taste, except populin, glycyrrhizin, and stevioside. 
 
Odor: Odorless, except saponins (glycyrrhizin). 
 
Hydrolysis: Can be hydrolyzed by mineral acids, temperature, or enzymes. 
 
Solubility of Components: Glycone part is soluble in water and insoluble in organic solvents. 
Aglycone part is insoluble in water and soluble in organic solvents. 
 
Effect of Sugar Residues: A glycoside with more sugar residues has greater solubility in water. 
 
Occurrence: Many glycosides occur in plants, often as flower and fruit pigments (e.g., 
anthocyanins). 
III. Types of Glycosides (Based on Linking Atom) 
 
O-Glycosides: Sugar and non-sugar moieties linked through an oxygen atom. 
o Examples: Digtoxin, digoxin, rhein-8-glucoside, sennosides A, B, C & D, salicin, arbutin, rutin, 
hesperidin, glycyrrhizin, dioscin, ginsenosides, prunasin, amygdalin. 
 
S-Glycosides: Sugar and non-sugar moieties linked through a sulfur atom. 
o Examples: Sinigrin, sinalbin, gluconapin. 
 
C-Glycosides: Sugar and non-sugar moieties linked without any heteroatom (directly carbon-to-
carbon). 
o Examples: Barbaloin, chrysaloin, cascarosides A, B, C & D. 
 
N-Glycosides: Sugar and non-sugar moieties linked through a nitrogen atom. 
o Examples: Nucleotides of adenine, guanine, cytosine, thiamine, and uracil (components of 
DNA & RNA).

Cardiotonic glycosides are a class of glycosides that exert a positive inotropic effect on cardiac muscles, 
meaning they increase the force of heart contractions. Crude drugs containing these glycosides include 
Digitalis (digitoxin), Digitalis lanata (digoxin), Strophanthus (K-strophanthin-β), Nerium oleander 
(Oleandrin), and white squill (scillaren-A). 
Classification of Cardiotonic Glycosides On the basis of their steroidal aglycone moiety, cardiotonic 
glycosides are divided into two main groups: 
 
Cardenolides: These have α, β-unsaturated five-membered lactone ring attached at the 17th 
position of the steroidal nucleus. Crude drugs containing cardenolides include Digitalis (digitoxin, 
digoxin), Digitalis lanata (digoxin), Strophanthus (K-strophanthin-β), and Nerium oleander 
(Oleandrin). 
 
Bufadienolides: These possess a doubly unsaturated six-membered lactone ring attached at the 
17th position of the steroidal nucleus. The crude drug containing bufadienolides is White squill 
(scillaren-A).

o Commercial Production: Digitalis purpurea seeds are sown in greenhouses in March, and

3. Anthraquinone Glycosides 
Anthraquinone glycosides yield anthraquinone as an aglycone upon hydrolysis. The free anthraquinone 
aglycones exhibit little therapeutic activity, while the sugar residue enhances its absorption and action. 
These glycosides are known as stimulant cathartics, increasing the tone and motion of the smooth 
muscles in the large intestine.
