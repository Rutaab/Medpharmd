# Basic Spectroscopy
Spectroscopy is broadly defined as the branch of science that studies the interaction of matter with light. Historically, it was understood as the "study of specific values or numbers" related to EMR. Alternatively, it can be defined as the study of the interaction of electromagnetic radiation (EMR) with matter.
Electromagnetic Radiation consists of discrete packages of energy called photons. Each photon comprises an oscillating electric field (E) and an oscillating magnetic field (M), which are perpendicular to each other.
It is important to differentiate between several related terms:
Spectroscopy: The general study of the interaction between matter and radiant energy.
Spectrometry: Involves the measurement of a spectrum where the spectrum is not necessarily formed by the emission or absorption of electromagnetic radiations, such as in Mass Spectrometry, which deals with fragments of masses.
Spectrophotometry: The study of a portion of the spectrum formed by EMR, where there is emission or absorption of light in the UV, visible, or IR regions.
## Principle
The principle of spectroscopy is based on the measurement of the spectrum of a sample containing atoms or molecules. A spectrum is typically presented as a graph of the intensity of absorbed or emitted radiation by a sample versus its frequency (ν) or wavelength (λ). The instrument designed for this measurement is called a spectrometer.
A spectrophotometer specifically measures the amount of EMR absorbed, emitted, or transmitted upon interaction with matter and consists of two main components:
A spectrometer (which generates EMR of a particular wavelength)
A photometer (which measures the intensity of radiation after interaction with matter).
There are two main types of spectroscopy based on the interaction measured:
Absorption Spectroscopy: This analytical technique measures the absorption of electromagnetic radiation by a sample. Examples include UV/Visible Spectroscopy (UV: 190-400 nm, Visible: 400-800 nm) and IR Spectroscopy (0.76-15 μm).
Emission Spectroscopy: This technique involves the dispersion of emission (of a particle or radiation) according to some property of the emission, and the amount of dispersion is measured. An example is ICP Spectroscopy.
