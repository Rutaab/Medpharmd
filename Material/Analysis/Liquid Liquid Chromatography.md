# Liquid Liquid Chromatography (LLC)
Liquid-Liquid Chromatography (LLC), also known as partition chromatography, is a technique where the stationary phase is a second liquid that is coated onto an inert solid surface and is immiscible with the solvent (mobile) phase.
## Principle and Mechanism
Separation is achieved by the partitioning of the sample between the two immiscible liquid phases, which causes some components to be retained longer than others.
This partitioning delays or retains certain components, leading to their separation.
## Advantages over Adsorption Chromatography
LLC is generally more reproducible and predictable.
The distribution coefficient remains constant over a much greater range of concentrations, resulting in sharper and more symmetrical peaks.
## Types of LLC
Normal Phase LLC: Uses a polar stationary phase and a non-polar mobile phase.
Reversed Phase LLC: Employs a nonpolar (hydrophobic) stationary phase and a polar liquid mobile phase (e.g., mixtures of water and methanol or acetonitrile). This mode works on the principle of hydrophobic interactions, meaning the more nonpolar the material, the longer it will be retained. Reversed phase mode has a wide range of pharmaceutical applications.
## Supports for Stationary Phase
Common materials used to support the stationary liquid phase include:
Silica gel.
Kieselguhr / Celite.
Cellulose.
## Steps in Liquid-Liquid Chromatography
The process generally involves:
Sample preparation.
Sample loading.
Elution: The mobile phase carries the separated components through the column.
Detection: Chemical methods and diverse types of detectors can be used to monitor the separated components.
Both LSC and LLC fall under the broader category of liquid-column chromatography, which uses glass or plastic columns, may require manual solvent flow (gravity, suction), and involves manual collection and analysis of fractions. These classical techniques have limitations such as tedious column packing, low column efficiency, long analysis times, and user dependency. Modern advancements, like High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC), address many of these issues, offering improved efficiency and automation.
