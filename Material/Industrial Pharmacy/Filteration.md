# Filtration

**Filtration** is a process used to separate a solid from a liquid or a gas by passing it through a porous material (a filter) that traps the solid but lets the fluid go through. When only a very small amount of solid is being removed, the process is often called **clarification**.

## Mechanism of filtration

The way a filter actually catches these particles is known as the **mechanism of filtration**, and there are two primary ways this happens:

### 1. Sieving Action (Cake Filtration)

This is the most straightforward type of filtration, similar to using a kitchen strainer.

**How it works:** It occurs when the particles in the liquid are **larger than the pores (holes)** of the filter. Because they cannot fit through the holes, the solids stay on the surface of the filter.

**Formation of a "Cake":** As more and more solids are trapped, they build up into a thick layer on top of the filter. This layer is called a **filter cake**, which is why this method is commonly known as **cake filtration**.

**The Cake as a Filter:** Interestingly, once this cake forms, its own surface begins to act as the filter medium. New solids are deposited on top of the existing cake, making it thicker over time. The liquid then has to travel through the tiny spaces (interstices) between the particles in the bulky cake. 

### 2. Depth Filtration

In this method, particles are trapped **inside the body** of the filter material rather than just on the surface.

**Construction:** These filters are usually made of fibrous or grainy materials arranged so that the liquid has to travel a long, winding path. A common example is **sheet filtration**, which uses a series of pads made from materials like asbestos fibers embedded in cellulose.* *

**How it works:** The particles being removed are actually **smaller than the average size of the holes** (voids) in the filter. Instead of being blocked by size, they are caught as they travel through the "depth" of the filter material.

**When it is used:** Depth filtration is typically used to remove small amounts of contaminants from very large volumes of liquid.

**Capture Mechanisms:** Since the particles could technically fit through the holes, they must be "captured" by sticking to the filter fibers. This happens through: 

**Physical Forces:** Particles are held by **van der Waals forces** or **electrical forces** once they get very close to the filter's solid surface.

**Diffusion:** Very tiny particles (smaller than 1*µ*m) move randomly and eventually bump into and stick to the filter surface.

**Impingement and Inertia:** Larger particles (around 20*µ*m or more) have enough weight and momentum that they cannot follow the curving path of the liquid; instead, they crash into a filter fiber and get stuck.

The rate at which a liquid passes through a filter is not constant; it changes based on several physical conditions. Scientists use mathematical formulas like **Poiseuille's Law** and the **Kozeny-Carman Equation** to predict and control this speed.

**Ultra-Filtration**

The process of separation of inter-micellar liquid from solid using pressure on a semi-permeable membrane. 

## Factors Affecting the Rate of Filtration

Four main factors determine how fast or slow a liquid (the filtrate) will flow through a filter:

**Filter Surface Area:** The larger the surface of the filter, the faster the liquid can pass through. In industrial settings, machines like filter presses are used specifically because they provide a massive surface area in a small space.

**Pressure Difference:** To move liquid through a filter, you need a "push" (positive pressure) or a "pull" (vacuum). Generally, increasing the pressure increases the flow rate. However, if the solids being filtered are "squishy" (compressible), too much pressure can crush them together and actually block the flow.

**Viscosity of the Liquid:** Viscosity refers to how "thick" a liquid is (like honey vs. water). Thicker liquids flow much slower. One common trick to speed up filtration is to heat the liquid, which makes it thinner and less viscous.

**Resistance of the Cake and Filter:** At the start, the liquid flows quickly because the filter is clean. As solids build up into a **filter cake**, they create resistance. The thicker the cake becomes, the harder it is for the liquid to squeeze through the tiny gaps between the particles.

**Poiseuille's Law**

To understand filtration mathematically, we can imagine that the tiny gaps between particles in a filter cake act like a bunch of very small, narrow tubes (capillaries). Poiseuille's Law describes how liquids flow through these types of tubes:

***V***** (Volume):** The amount of liquid that passes through.

**P (Pressure):** Higher pressure leads to more volume.

**r (Radius):** This is the size of the "holes" in the cake. Because it is raised to the 4th power ($r^4$), even a tiny increase in the size of the gaps makes the filtration much faster.

***η***** (Viscosity):** The "thickness" of the liquid. Higher viscosity reduces the flow.

**l (Length/Thickness):** This represents the thickness of the filter cake. As the cake gets thicker, the liquid has a longer path to travel, which slows down the flow.

## Filter Media & Filter Aids

In pharmaceutical manufacturing, choosing the right tools to separate solids from liquids is essential for creating pure and safe products*.*

### Filter Media

The **filter medium** is the actual porous material (the "barrier") that performs the separation by trapping solids while allowing the liquid or gas to pass through.

**Ideal Characteristics of a Filter Medium**

When selecting a medium, pharmacists look for specific qualities to ensure the process is efficient and doesn't ruin the product:

**Chemically Inert:** It should not react with the liquid or change physically (like shrinking) when it gets wet.

**Efficient:** It should allow the maximum amount of liquid to pass through while still catching all the solid particles.

**Strong:** It must have enough mechanical strength to withstand the pressure of the filtration process without tearing or breaking.

**Non-Absorbent:** It should not soak up or "steal" important dissolved ingredients from the medicine.

**Common Examples of Filter Media**

**Filter Paper:** The most common medium for small-scale work. It comes in different "textures" and "porosities" (hole sizes) depending on what needs to be filtered.

**Sintered Glass:** Made by fusing glass granules into a plate. These are excellent for filtering corrosive liquids or medicines that need to be injected (parenterals) because they don't shed fibers into the liquid.

**Membrane Filters:** Very thin plastics with uniform hole sizes. They work about 40 times faster than conventional filters and are used to create "particulate-free" solutions for eyes or injections.

**Synthetic Fabrics:** Materials like **Nylon** or **Terylene** are used in industrial machines because they are stronger and more resistant to chemicals than natural fibers like wool or cotton.

### Filter Aids

Sometimes, a liquid is very difficult to filter because it contains "slimy" or "squishy" (compressible) materials that immediately clog the holes of the filter medium. In these cases, a **filter aid** is used.

**How They Work**

A filter aid is a gritty, porous powder that builds a rigid "lattice" or framework. This framework traps the slimy solids but leaves plenty of open channels for the liquid to flow through, preventing the filter from becoming a "blocked" mess.

**Methods of Use**

Filter aids are typically used in one of two ways:

**Direct Addition:** The aid is stirred directly into the liquid before filtration starts.

**Pre-coating:** A layer of the filter aid is deposited onto the filter medium first to act as a protective "shield" before the liquid is poured through.

**Important Qualities and Examples**

To be effective, a filter aid should be **lightweight (low specific gravity)**, **highly porous**, and **chemically inert**.

Commonly used filter aids include:

**Kieselguhr (Diatomaceous Earth):** One of the most popular choices because it is very porous.

**Charcoal:** Often used for its ability to remove color (decolorizing), though it must be used carefully because it can also "trap" and remove active medicinal ingredients like alkaloids.

**Other materials:** Talc, asbestos, paper pulp, bentonite, and Fuller's earth.

## Pretreatment

In pharmaceutical manufacturing, some materials are almost impossible to filter in their natural state. **Pretreatment** involves modifying these materials through various physical or chemical means to make the filtration process successful.

### Heat Treatment

Temperature can significantly change the physical properties of a substance, making it easier or harder to filter.

**Creating Coarser Particles:** When a solid is precipitated (turned into a solid from a liquid solution), doing so at a **high temperature** in a concentrated solution creates larger, "coarser" particles. These large particles are much easier for a filter to catch than the tiny particles formed in cold, dilute solutions.

**Removing Unwanted Proteins:** In vegetable drug extracts, heating the liquid causes unwanted proteins to clump together, a process called **coagulation**. Once these proteins have clumped into larger masses, they can be easily filtered off.

**Managing Waxes:** Waxes behave differently. When they are warm, they become soft and "slimy," which can easily clog a filter. To filter them effectively, they must be **chilled** until they become hard and rigid particles.

### Removal of Tannins

Tannins are natural compounds often found in plant-based medicines that can make alcoholic preparations look cloudy or "turbid".

**The Process:** To clear this cloudiness, manufacturers add substances like **gelatin** or **isinglass**.

**The Result:** These additives chemically bond with the tannins to form an **insoluble complex**--essentially a solid mass. Because this mass is now a solid and won't dissolve, it can be simply filtered out of the liquid.

### pH Effects

The acidity or alkalinity (the pH level) of a solution has a major impact on whether a substance stays dissolved or turns into a solid that can be filtered.

**Isoelectric Point:** This is particularly important for proteins. Every protein has a specific pH level called its **isoelectric point**.

**Maximum Precipitation:** At this specific pH, the protein is **least soluble**, meaning it is most likely to turn into a solid precipitate. By adjusting the liquid to this exact pH, manufacturers can force the protein into a solid, "filterable" form.

In pharmaceutical operations, **filtration** is primarily used to separate and remove small amounts of unwanted matter, such as bacteria, from relatively large volumes of liquid. Unlike many chemical processes that aim to isolate crystals, pharmaceutical filtration often focuses on **clarification** to ensure the final product is pure and safe.

## Equipment Used in Filtration

Below is a detailed look at the methods and equipment used in filtration, organized by their construction and application.

### 1. Filter Funnels (Buchner and Hirsch Funnels)

**Introduction:** These are the most common tools for small-scale filtration in a laboratory setting.

**Construction:** These funnels are usually made of **glass or porcelain**. A **Buchner funnel** features a flat, perforated plate to support a filter disc, while a **Hirsch funnel** is a smaller, modified version for tiny amounts of material. They are used in conjunction with a "pressure flask" (a thick glass container with a side arm).

**Working:** A piece of filter paper is placed on the plate. The funnel is fitted into the flask with a rubber stopper, and a vacuum pump is attached to the flask's side arm to pull the liquid through the paper.

**Advantages & Disadvantages:** They are simple and effective for small batches. However, they are not suitable for large-scale industrial use.

**Limitations:** A major risk is **"back suction,"** where a drop in water pressure can cause water to be sucked from the pump back into the flask, contaminating the filtrate.

**Ways to Overcome:** To prevent this, an **intermediate flask** is placed between the pump and the collection flask to catch any sucked-back water.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Used for small-scale laboratory preparations and testing the porosity of filter paper.

### 2. Sintered Glass Filters

**Introduction:** These are filters where the filtering medium is made of fused glass particles.

**Construction:** They are made from Pyrex or Jena glass that has been powdered, sifted to a uniform size, and then molded into flat or convex plates. These plates are then fused into glass funnels of various shapes.

**Working:** They operate similarly to Buchner funnels but are often used under reduced pressure.

**Advantages & Disadvantages:** They do not shed any fibers into the liquid and are highly resistant to chemicals.

**Limitations:** They can become clogged over time, especially with fine particles.

**Ways to Overcome:** They can be cleaned by gently scrubbing with a soft brush or by **reverse-flushing** with water or chemicals. For thick (viscous) liquids, they can be surrounded by a heating jacket.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Ideal for filtering **corrosive liquids**, oxidizing agents, parenteral (injectable) solutions, and eye lotions.

### 3. Seitz Filters

**Introduction:** These filters are specifically designed for clarification and sterilization of small quantities of liquid.

**Construction:** The unit consists of glass parts held together by metal clamping rings and screws. It uses an **asbestos filter sheet** supported by a perforated stainless steel disc.

**Working:** The entire apparatus is assembled, wrapped, and sterilized in an autoclave before use. The liquid only comes into contact with the glass and the stainless steel disc.

**Advantages & Disadvantages:** It is easy to clean and sterilize, and there is **no risk of metal poisoning** because the liquid touches only inert materials.

**Limitations:** It is primarily meant for small volumes.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Used for producing sterile solutions and clarifying liquids that must remain free of contamination.

### 4. Membrane Filters

**Introduction:** These are extremely thin, porous sheets made from cellulose esters or similar plastic materials.

**Construction:** They feature very uniform pores that go straight through the membrane. They come in various sizes, from small discs to large multiplate units that can hold up to 60 discs at once.

**Working:** They act like a sieve, trapping particles on the surface. Because the pores are so tiny, they are almost always operated under **pressure** (either pushing the liquid through or pulling it with a vacuum).

**Advantages & Disadvantages:** They work about **40 times faster** than traditional filters and do not shed fibers. However, they can clog quickly.

**Limitations:** They are very **brittle when dry**, making them difficult to handle, and can rupture if pressure fluctuates.

**Ways to Overcome:** To prevent clogging, a **prefilter** (like a depth filter) is often used to catch large chunks first. For durability, the membrane is often held between layers of synthetic fabric in a **cartridge form**.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Essential for making "particulate-free" solutions for eyes (ophthalmic) or injections (parenteral), and for vaccine production.

### 5. Glass Fibre Paper

**Introduction:** This is a filter medium made of straight glass fibers that have a uniform thickness.

**Construction:** It is manufactured into sheets with a range of pore sizes; finer papers use thinner fibers.

**Working:** This is a form of **depth filtration**, meaning particles are trapped inside the thickness of the fibers rather than just on the surface.

**Advantages & Disadvantages:** It has higher flow rates and better trapping efficiency than standard membrane or cellulose papers. It also does not swell when wet.

**Limitations:** Its useful life can be short if the pores clog rapidly.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Used when high flow rates are needed for liquids that might otherwise clog a surface filter.

### 6. Filter Press

**Introduction:** A widely used industrial machine that is compact and economical.

**Construction:** It consists of a series of square units (plates and frames) held together by a large screw. Filter cloth is clamped between these units.

**Working:** The liquid (slurry) is pumped into the frames under pressure. The liquid passes through the cloth and exits through ports, while the solids stay inside the frames to form a "cake".

**Advantages & Disadvantages:** 

Advantages: Simple construction, no moving parts, large filter area in a small space, and produces a dry solid cake.

Disadvantages: High operating costs (it takes labor to set up and clean), and washing the solids is not very efficient.

**Limitations:** Frequent dismantling for cleaning leads to wear and tear on the filter medium.

**Ways to Overcome:** Taps can be fitted to each plate so that if one section fails, it can be turned off without stopping the whole machine.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Used for large-scale clarification or removing solid phases from a liquid.

### 7. Metafilter

**Introduction:** A specialized clarifying filter that is built to fit the specific size of the batch being processed.

**Construction:** It consists of a column of **rings** with tiny raised bumps on one side. When the rings are stacked, these bumps create tiny channels.

**Working:** A "filter bed" made of a powder (like charcoal or kieselguhr) is deposited onto the rings. Liquid is then forced through this bed and into the channels.

**Advantages & Disadvantages:** It is very easy to clean by **back-flushing** (forcing liquid or steam through the opposite way), which washes away the entire filter bed instantly.

**Limitations:** It requires a filter aid to work effectively.

**Ways to Overcome:** It can be fitted with a **steam jacket** to help filter very thick oils or syrups by keeping them warm.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Excellent for clarifying syrups, oils, or moderate-sized batches where frequent cleaning is needed.

### 8. Drum Filter (Rotary Vacuum Filter)

**Introduction:** A large-scale industrial filter that operates continuously.

**Construction:** It is a large metal cylinder that rotates horizontally. The surface is perforated and covered with a filter cloth.

**Working:** As the drum rotates through a tub of slurry, a vacuum inside the drum pulls the liquid in. The solids stick to the outside, forming a cake. This cake is washed, dried with air, and then scraped off by a **"doctor knife"**.

**Advantages & Disadvantages:** It is a continuous process, meaning you don't have to stop it to remove the cake. It can produce a cake that is almost completely dry.

**Limitations:** It requires complex vacuum systems and specialized maintenance.

**Ways to Overcome:** The speed of rotation can be adjusted based on the material being filtered to ensure the best results.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Used for high-volume industrial filtration where the solid cake needs to be recovered or discarded efficiently.

**Air filtration** is a critical part of the air conditioning systems used in pharmaceutical factories. Its main purpose is to create either a **dust-free environment** for general work or a **sterile atmosphere** for aseptic (germ-free) processes. Unlike liquid filters, air filters must have an "open" construction to allow high flow rates, meaning the holes are actually much larger than the particles they are trying to catch.

**Mechanisms of Air Filtration**

Because the holes in an air filter are relatively large, particles aren't caught by a "sieving" action; instead, they are removed when they accidentally collide with and stick to the filter material. This sticking is caused by physical forces like **van der Waals forces** or electrical charges. There are two main ways these collisions happen:

**Impingement (For Larger Particles):** This happens when air is moving at **high speeds**. As the air flows around a filter fiber, the "streamlines" (the path of the air) bend to get around it. Larger particles (5$\mu$m or bigger) have too much momentum to follow these sharp curves; they continue in a straight line, crash into the fiber, and get stuck.

**Diffusion (For Tiny Particles):** This is effective for very small particles (less than 1$\mu$m) and works best at **low air speeds**. These tiny particles move randomly in every direction (a process called Brownian motion). This random zig-zagging makes it very likely that they will eventually bump into a filter fiber and adhere to it.

**Methods of Air Filtration**

There are three primary methods used to clean the air in a pharmaceutical setting:

1. **Oiled Metal Plates or Mesh**
These are often called "viscous filters."

**How they work:** The air is passed over a large surface area of metal (like corrugated plates or wire mesh) that has been **coated in oil**.

**Design:** As the air hits the sticky, oiled surface, the dust particles get trapped in the oil. In large industrial models, there is a **rotary metal screen** that continuously passes through a trough of oil to stay coated and clean.

2. **Dry Type (Fibrous or Granular Material)**
This is a very common method for general air cleaning.

**How they work:** These filters consist of frames packed with **fibrous materials** like cotton wool, glass wool, slag wool, or asbestos.

**Maintenance:** Generally, these filter frames are **discarded** once they get too dirty, although some types are designed so they can be cleaned and reused.

3. **Electrostatic Precipitation**
While more expensive than the other methods, this is **extremely efficient**, catching up to 98% of dust.

**How it works:** This method uses electricity to act like a magnet for dust. Incoming air passes through a series of wires with a very high electrical charge (about 12 kV), which **ionizes** the dust particles (gives them an electrical charge).

**Capture:** The charged dust particles are then pulled toward "Collector" plates that have the opposite charge (about 7 kV). The dust sticks to these plates, which are periodically cleaned by spraying them with water.

**Testing Filter Efficiency**

To ensure an air filter is working correctly, experts use a **photometer** to compare the amount of dust in the air before and after it passes through the filter. For **bacteria-proof filters**, they test the air using a "slit sampler" to count how many germs are actually removed from a specially "infected" air sample.
