**Drying**

In pharmaceutical manufacturing, **drying** is defined as the removal of a liquid from a material by applying heat, which transforms the liquid into an **unsaturated vapor phase**.

While this usually refers to removing small amounts of water, it is distinguished from evaporation by the relative quantity of liquid removed; evaporation typically involves removing large volumes of water. There are also non-thermal methods, such as **desiccation** (using silica gel in a sealed container) or **expression** (squeezing a solid to remove liquid).

**Purpose of Drying**

Drying is a critical unit process for several reasons:

**Preparation of Granules:** Essential for making tablets and capsules.

**Stability and Preservation:** Dried products are generally more stable, as drying reduces chemical reactivity and prevents the growth of mold and bacteria in moisture-sensitive animal or vegetable drugs.

**Processing Efficiency:** Drying makes substances more **friable** (brittle), which makes them easier to grind or pulverize.

**Cost Reduction:** Removing moisture reduces the bulk and weight of a product, lowering transportation and storage costs.

**Fundamental Theory: Heat and Mass Transfer**

The drying process is governed by two simultaneous operations: **heat transfer** and **mass transfer**.

**Heat Transfer:** Heat must be moved to the material to provide the "latent heat of vapourization" required to turn the liquid into vapor. This can happen via **convection** (hot air), **radiation** (heat lamps), or **conduction** (direct contact with a hot surface).

**Mass Transfer:** This involves the movement (diffusion) of water through the material to its surface, followed by the evaporation of that water into the surrounding air stream.

The **rate of drying** can be accelerated by increasing the air flow rate, raising the inlet air temperature, reducing the thickness of the material, or dehumidifying the inlet air.

**Measuring Moisture: LOD vs. MC**

In pharmacy, moisture is measured in two ways:

**Loss on Drying (LOD):** Calculated on a **wet-weight basis**. It is the weight of water in a sample divided by the total weight of the wet sample, multiplied by 100.

**Moisture Content (MC):** Calculated on a **dry-weight basis**. It is the weight of water divided by the weight of the dry solid, multiplied by 100.

Additionally, pharmacists distinguish between **Loss on Drying (LOD)**, which calculates moisture as a percentage of the **wet weight**, and **Moisture Content (MC)**, which uses the **dry weight**. MC is considered a more realistic value for determining dryer load capacity because it more accurately reflects the amount of water that must be evaporated per pound of dry product.

**Equilibrium Moisture Content (EMC):** The moisture content at which a material is in perfect equilibrium with its environment. At this point, the drying rate is zero, and further drying is a waste of energy.

**Free Water:** Any moisture present in excess of the EMC.

**Bound Water:** Water held in small capillary pores or adsorbed on the surface. This water exerts a lower vapor pressure than pure water.

**Water Activity (a**~**w**~**):** The ratio of the vapor pressure of the material to the vapor pressure of pure water. It is a key measure for determining chemical stability and the potential for microbial growth.

**The Stages of Drying (Drying Rate Curve)**

When a solid dries, it moves through several distinct stages, often visualized as a "drying rate curve":

**Initial Adjustment (Segment AB):** The material absorbs heat and stabilizes at the **wet-bulb temperature** of the drying air.

**Constant Rate Period (Segment BC):** A film of moisture remains on the surface of the solid. As water evaporates, it is immediately replaced by water diffusing from the interior. The drying rate remains steady.

**Critical Moisture Content (Point C):** This is the point where the surface water is no longer replaced fast enough to maintain a continuous film. **Dry spots** begin to appear.

**First Falling Rate Period (Segment CD):** The number of dry spots grows, and the drying rate begins to drop steadily.

**Second Falling Rate Period (Segment DE):** The surface film is completely gone. Drying now depends entirely on how fast vapor can diffuse through the pores of the solid.

**Equilibrium Moisture Content (EMC):** The final stage where the material's vapor pressure equals that of the surrounding atmosphere, and drying stops.

**Behavior of Different Solids**

The ease of drying depends on the type of solid:

**Granular or Crystalline Solids:** These hold water in shallow, open pores. They are easy to dry, have a long constant-rate period, and their EMC is near zero.

**Amorphous Solids:** These include fibrous or gelatinous materials like starch, insulin, or yeast. The moisture is part of their molecular structure, making movement very slow. They are **much more difficult to dry** and often require reduced pressure or specialized temperatures.

**Drying Equipments:**

Drying apparatuses in pharmaceutical manufacturing are primarily classified by their **method of solids handling**, which determines how the material interacts with the heating medium and moves through the system. These systems are categorized into four major types: **static-bed, moving-bed, fluidized-bed, and pneumatic dryers**.

1. **Static-Bed Systems**
In these systems, there is no relative movement among the solid particles being dried, though the entire mass may move.

**A. Tray and Truck Dryers**

**Introduction:** These are the most common dryers in pharmaceutical plants. A tray dryer uses a cabinet with tiers of trays, while a truck dryer uses racks on wheels for easier loading.

**Mechanism:** They operate primarily through **forced convection**, where large volumes of heated air are circulated over the material.

**Construction:** These consist of a well-insulated cabinet with heaters (steam or electric), fans to circulate air, and adjustable louvers.

**Working:** Material is spread on trays (0.5 to 4 inches deep). Heated air circulates at 200 to 2,000 feet per minute.

**Limitation:** They provide a low drying rate and are labor-intensive. They can also suffer from non-uniform airflow and "stagnant pockets".

**How to Overcome:** Using **adjustable louvers** helps eliminate stagnant pockets and ensures more uniform airflow.

**Advantages:** They allow each batch to be handled as a separate entity and are easily adjusted for a wide variety of materials.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** General drying of powders and chemicals where batch sizes are relatively small.

**B. Vacuum Shelf Dryers**

**Introduction:** These are similar to tray dryers but operate under low pressure, making them an alternative for small quantities of sensitive material.

**Mechanism:** By reducing pressure below the saturation pressure of the solvent, the boiling point of the liquid is lowered, causing rapid vaporization.

**Construction:** Heavy-duty cabinets with hollow shelves that can be heated by steam or hot water.

**Working:** Material is placed on shelves, the chamber is sealed, and a vacuum is applied. Heat is transferred by conduction from the shelves to the material.

**Limitation:** They require massive construction and have high costs and long drying times (12 to 48 hours).

**Advantages:** Ideal for **low-temperature drying** of heat-sensitive (thermolabile) materials and for excluding oxygen to prevent oxidation.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Drying **penicillin** and materials requiring solvent recovery.

******C. Freeze Dryers (Lyophilization)**

**Introduction:** Used for products that lose viability or deteriorate if dried in air at normal pressures.

**Mechanism:** Based on **sublimation**, where water passes directly from a solid state (ice) to a vapor state without becoming a liquid.

**Construction:** Includes a vacuum chamber, a vacuum source, a heat source (conduction or radiation), and a vapor-removal system (condenser).

**Working:** Material is pre-frozen (below -20°C). A high vacuum is applied, and heat is added to sublimate the ice (primary drying). Finally, the temperature is raised to remove bound water (secondary drying).

**Limitation:** It is an expensive and complex process.

**Advantages:** Stabilizes highly sensitive biological materials that cannot withstand heat.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** **Blood serum, plasma, antibiotics, vaccines, and hormones**.

**D. Tunnel and Conveyor Dryers**

**Introduction:** These are adaptations of truck dryers for continuous or semi-continuous operation.

**Mechanism:** Convection-based drying.

**Construction:** A long tunnel through which trucks (tunnel dryer) or an endless belt (conveyor dryer) moves the material.

**Working:** In a conveyor dryer, wet material is loaded onto a belt that carries it through a tunnel where heated air is circulated.

**Limitation:** Drying rate curves for batch drying are not equally applicable here because air conditions change as the material moves along the path.

**Advantages:** Conveyor dryers are truly **continuous** and suitable for handling large volumes of material.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Large-scale manufacturing of uniform materials.

2. **Moving-Bed Systems**
In these systems, particles are partially separated so that they flow over each other, creating new surfaces for faster heat transfer.

**A. Vacuum Tumble Dryers**

**Introduction:** Often double-cone shaped, these mixers are designed to overcome the long drying times of static dryers.

**Mechanism:** Combined conduction and heat transfer through vapor under vacuum.

**Construction:** A rotating double-cone shell with a jacket for heating fluid.

**Working:** The shell rotates (6-8 rpm) while heat is applied through the jacket walls. The tumbling action constantly exposes new material to the heat.

**Limitation:** Waxy solids may aggregate into balls. Larger models may have less jacket area relative to the volume of material.

**How to Overcome:** Larger models can use **internal plates or pipe coils** to increase the surface area for heat transfer.

**Advantages:** Faster than static ovens and produces a **uniform powder** rather than a cake.

******Pharmaceutical Applications:** Drying **tablet granules**.

**B. Pan Dryers**

**Introduction:** These are indirect-type dryers used for small batches.

**Mechanism:** Conduction.

**Construction:** A shallow, circular jacketed pan with rotating plows.

**Working:** Plows rotate slowly, scraping the material from the walls and exposing new surfaces to the heated bottom.

**Advantages:** Can operate under a vacuum for **solvent recovery**.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Pastes and slurries.

**C. Rotary Dryers**

******Introduction:** A modified form of a tunnel dryer where material is actively turned.

**Mechanism:** Convection.

**Construction:** A sloped rotating cylindrical shell with internal baffles or "flights".

**Working:** Material is passed through the rotating cylinder against a stream of heated air. The baffles turn the material over as it moves.

**Advantages:** Continuous operation with better drying rates than static beds.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Large volumes of solid materials.

******D. Turbo-Tray Dryers**

**Introduction:** A continuous, moving-bed system featuring vertical stacks of trays.

**Mechanism:** Convection.

**Construction:** Rotating annular trays in a vertical stack with stationary wipers and fans.

**Working:** Material is fed to the top tray, leveled, and then wiped through slots to the tray below. This repeats until the material is discharged at the bottom.

**Advantages:** Faster drying rates than tunnel dryers because it **continuously exposes new surfaces**.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Continuous processing of solids.

******E. ****Drum Dryers**

**Introduction:** These use rotating cylinders to dry solutions or slurries.

**Mechanism:** Drying occurs through **simple evaporation**.

**Construction:** One or two steam-heated cylinders that rotate slowly, a feed trough, and a scraper blade.

**Working:** The rotating drum is coated with the liquid feed. As it turns, the liquid evaporates, and the dried solid is scraped off by the blade.

**Limitation:** They can subject solids to greater heat treatment than spray dryers.

**Advantages:** They offer a **short contact time**, which is advantageous for some thermolabile materials.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Drying **kaolin or zinc oxide** suspensions.

3. **Fluidized-Bed Systems**
Particles are partially suspended in an upward-moving gas stream, behaving like a "boiling liquid".

******A. Vertical Fluidized-Bed Dryers**

**Introduction:** These systems offer intense mixing and uniform conditions.

**Mechanism:** Excellent gas-solid contact for high heat and mass transfer.

**Construction:** A container with a wire mesh bottom, an air heater, a fan, and a bag collector filter.

**Working:** Heated air is blown upward through the material at a speed greater than the particles' settling velocity, causing them to "boil" in the air stream.

**Limitation:** Can build up **electrostatic charges**; overly friable material may produce fine particles through attrition.

**How to Overcome:** Grounding devices are used to avoid explosion hazards from static electricity.

**Advantages:** **2 to 6 times faster** and more efficient than tray dryers.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Drying and preparing **tablet granulations**.

**B. Horizontal Vibrating Conveyors**

**Introduction:** A continuous version of the fluidized-bed dryer suitable for large volumes.

**Mechanism:** Combined fluidization and vibration.

**Construction:** A horizontal vibrating deck with multiple zones for independent airflow and temperature control.

**Working:** Material is moved along the vibrating deck while heated air is passed up through it from a chamber below.

**Advantages:** High capacity (up to **2 tons per hour**) and allows for specialized drying zones. Dryers vary in width from 12 to 57 inches and in length from 10 to 50 feet, with bed depths of 3 inches. Dryer capacity is limited only by the retention time produced by conveying speeds, which range from 5 to 25 feet per minute.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Large-volume pharmaceutical operations.

4. **Pneumatic Dryers**
In these dryers, particles are entrained (carried) and conveyed in a high-velocity gas stream.

**A. Spray Dryers**

**Introduction:** Specifically designed to handle fluid materials like solutions and slurries.

**Mechanism:** Extremely rapid evaporation from fine droplets.

**Construction:** Includes a feed pump, an **atomizer** (pneumatic, nozzle, or spinning disc), a drying chamber, and a cyclone separator.

**Working:** Liquid is atomized into a stream of hot gas. The droplets dry into fine powder in seconds and are collected by the cyclone.

**Limitation:** Viscous liquids can clog some atomizers.

**How to Overcome:** Use **spinning disc atomizers** to handle high-viscosity liquids and slurries that would clog other types.

**Advantages:** Rapid drying (seconds) keeps the product cool; produces **uniform, spherical particles** with excellent flow properties.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Spray-dried **lactose**, vitamins, and encapsulating "dry" flavor oils.

**B. Flash Dryers**

**Introduction:** These provide extremely short drying times (seconds).

**Mechanism:** Attrition and convection in high-velocity air.

**Construction:** A duct system with high-velocity air (3,000-6,000 ft/min) and a cyclone separator.

**Working:** Moistened solid is suspended in the hot air stream. The high velocity causes particles to strike each other, exposing new surfaces for instant drying.

**Advantages:** Very fast; the temperature of the drying solid can be kept very low (below 100°F) despite high air temperatures.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Drying friable materials where particle size reduction is also desired.

5. **Specialized Drying: Microwave Drying**
**Introduction:** A radical departure that uses microwave energy instead of external heat.

**Mechanism:** Microwaves interact with polarized molecules to generate **internal heat**.

**Construction:** Typically a static-bed continuous type with a conveyor belt and microwave applicators.

**Working:** Microwaves generate internal vapor pressure that mobilizes moisture to the surface rapidly. A stream of hot air is used simultaneously to sweep the vapor away.

**Limitation:** High initial interest is offset by energy costs, though it is highly efficient.

**Advantages:** Avoids "case hardening" (where the surface gets hard and traps moisture inside), saves up to **70% energy**, and reduces total drying time by 50% or more.

**Pharmaceutical Applications:** Drying **vitamins, enzymes, and proteins** at low ambient temperatures.
