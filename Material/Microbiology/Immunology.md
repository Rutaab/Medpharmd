# Immunology


“The branch of medicine and biology concerned with immunity.”

### Introduction:

Immunology charts, measures, and contextualizes the physiological functioning of the immune system in states of both health and diseases; malfunctions of the immune system in immunological disorders (such as autoimmune diseases, hypersensitivities, immune deficiency, and transplant rejection); and the physical, chemical, and physiological characteristics of the components of the immune system in vitro , in situ , and in vivo .

## Host Defenses:

Immunology is the study of the specific defenses of a host against diseases. In addition to these specific immune responses, host defenses also include non-specific resistance. Acting together, the components of the immune system work to protect the host against infections.

### 1. Non-Specific Defenses:

The human body has many non-specific defenses to prevent disease. These include:

**i.** First Line Defense

**ii.** Phagocytosis

**iii.** Inflammation

**iv.** Complement System

### i. First Line Defense:

Several components make up the first line of defense.

The skin and mucous membranes serve as physical barriers to prevent microorganisms and harmful substances from entering the human body.

The very high acidity of gastric juices can destroy many pathogens.

Tears prevent the microorganism from growing on the surfaces of eye-ball.

Tears, perspiration and saliva contains lysozyme enzymes which breakdown the cell-wall of bacteria.

First Line Defenses also include proteins such as the iron binding trans-ferans which inhibit bacterial growth by reducing the amount of available Iron in blood.

### ii. Phagocytosis:

“ Phagocytosis is the ingestion of a solid such as a microorganism or cellular debris by a eukaryotic cell.” Phagocytosis is used by some protozoans for obtaining nutrients, but this process is also used by certain cells of immune system to fight. Certain immune system cells such as macrophages and neutrophils use phagocytosis to ingest and destroy the invading microorganisms. These phagocytes attack and destroy invaders using chemotaxis, adherence, ingestion, digestion and elimination **a)** **Chemotaxis: ** “Chemotaxis is the movement of the cell toward or away from a chemical stimulus.” o Phagocytes use pseudopods to move towards microorganism or damaged cells at the site of infection. Often arriving only minutes after the infection. Chemotactic chemicals attract the phagocytes to the site of infection and aids in the adherence of phagocytes to the side of microorganisms. o Chemotactic chemicals that attract phagocytes include microbial products, components of damaged cells, chemicals released by other white blood cells and peptides derived from complement system. The complement system is another form of host defense. **b)** **Adherence: ** “The plasma membrane of phagocytes attaches to the surface of microorganisms or other foreign material, this process is called adherence.” o Phagocytes can adhere to certain components of microorganisms often interacting with glycoproteins. o This can be made easier through a process called opsonization in which the microorganisms are coded with serum proteins called opsonins. Opsonins function as handles for phagocytosis and include certain components of the compliment system as well as components of the humoral immune system called antibodies. **c)** **Ingestion: ** o During ingestion the macrophage expands pseudopods to surround and engulf the microorganism. o The pseudopods fuse and forming a sac called phagosome. **d)** **Digestion: ** o The phagosome enters the cytoplasm where it fuses with lysosomes which contain digestive enzymes and other antimicrobial substances. o The fused structure is called a phagolysosome. o Digestion of most bacteria is complete within 10 to 30 minutes.

**e)** **Elimination: ** “After phagocytosis the remaining debris is eliminated from the cell by exocytosis.” o In which the phagolysosome fuses with the plasma membrane and expels its contents. o Some of the contents of phagolysosome may also be presented on the cell membrane through a process called antigen processing and presentation. **Evasion of Adherence: ** o Some bacteria have structures that help them avoid adherence. For example, the protein of Streptococcus pyogenes and capsules of microorganisms such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Hemophilus influenzae type B inhibit the attachment of phagocyte to the surfaces of bacteria, making the bacteria slippery. These microorganisms can only be phagocytized if the phagocyte grabs the bacteria against the rough surface such as walls of the blood vessel, a connective tissue fiber or a blood clot. **Evasion of Digestion: ** o Some organisms are ingested but not killed. o Some attack the phagosome membrane allowing to escape from the phagosome to reproduce inside the phagocyte. They then escape from the phagocyte by using the same process on the cytoplasmic membrane. o Others prevents the phagosome from fusing with the lysosome. While others cause the release of phagocyte’s lysosomal enzyme into the cytoplasm killing the phagocyte. o Some microorganism such as Coxiela brunetii, the causative agent of Q-fever even require the low pH inside the phagolysosome to reproduce.

### iii. Inflammation:

“Damage to the body tissues through heat, chemicals, sun burns, cuts or microbial infections triggers a general non-specific defensive response called inflammation.” Signs and symptoms of inflammation include redness, pain, heat, swelling and sometimes loss of function. Inflammation has three main functions: o To destroy the agent causing injury. o To limit the effect of the agents on rest of the body. o To repair or replace the damaged tissue. Inflammation consists of the following stages: **a)** **Vasodilation: ** o Damaged cells release several chemicals such as histamines, prostaglandins and leukotrienes. Certain components of the complement system can also stimulate the release of histamines. o One function of these chemicals is to cause **vasodilation** . o Vasodilation results in more blood and therefore more phagocytes, oxygen and nutrients being delivered to the site of injury. This increased blood flow results in redness, heat, swelling and some of the pain associated with inflammation. **b)** **Phagocyte Migration: ** o The increased blood flow delivers phagocytes such as monocytes and neutrophils to the scene. The phagocytes stick to walls of blood vessels in the process called margination then squeeze through the gaps between the cells in a process called emigration or diapedesis. Within about hour of tissue damage the phagocytes begin to destroy the invading microorganisms by phagocytosis. After engulfing a large number of microorganisms and damaged tissue, the phagocytes die forming puss. **c)** **Tissue Repair: ** o The final stage of inflammation is tissue repair. o The increased delivery of nutrients and oxygen from vasodilation aids in this process. Other components of the blood lead to the formation of blood clot which seals off the site of injury. **d)** **Destruction of the Injury-causing Agent: ** o The migration of phagocytes and other factors to the site of injury usually results in the destruction of microorganism. Phagocytes directly attack the microorganisms but other components of the body’s defense systems such as the compliment system are involved. **e)** **Repairing and Replacing Damaged Tissue: ** o Inflammation leads to repair and replacement of damaged tissue when possible. Different tissues have different capacities to regenerate themselves. While the outer layer of skin is completely able to repair a small cut leaving no scar. Cardiac muscle tissue does not regenerate at all.

### iv. Complement System:

The compliment system is composed of several proteins that circulate in the blood serum. Activation of the compliment system initiates a cascade of events that aids in the destruction of invading microorganisms. The compliment system helps inducing the inflammatory response. It produces opsonin and chemotactic factors that aid in phagocytosis. Components of complement system also directly attack the cell membrane of microorganisms causing cytolysis.

### 2. Specific Defenses:

The human body has two main divisions of specific defense:

**i.** Humoral Immunity

**ii.** Cell-Mediated Immunity Antigen presenting cells aid in the activation of these defenses. Antigen processing and presentation is used by the host to provide a window into the cell for components of the immune system that are responsible for tracking down infected or cancerous cells in the body. Most nucleated cells present a sampling of the proteins produced inside them and specialized antigen presenting cells present peptides from the proteins of the engulfed invaders.

### i. Humoral Immunity:

Humoral Immune response consists of the activity of B-cells and the antibodies they produce. The process of B-cell activation is called clonal selection. **Process: ** o During this process a B-cell with a specific antigen receptor recognizes an antigen. And this cell proliferates to produce a multitude of cells also called a clone of cells with the same specificity. Which differentiates into memory B-cells and plasma cells. o The antibodies produced by the plasma cells bind to the antigens to produce the humoral response. **a)** **B-Cell Activation: **

In the simplest form of B-cell activation an antigen called a T-independent-antigen enters the lymphatic tissue and binds to an antigen receptor also called a B-Cell Receptor (BCR) on the surface of a B-cell.

These receptors are membrane bound versions of the antibodies that can be produced by that cell.

Each of the B-cell receptor on a B-cell is identical and binds specifically to one antigen. However, the body produces billions of different B-cells each of which produces a different receptor making it likely that atleast one B-cell will recognize the antigen.

**b)** **B-Cell Proliferation: **

Once a B-cell becomes activated, it proliferates or divides forming a clone of cells specific to the same antigen. While some of these B-cells differentiate into long lived memory B-cells, most differentiate into plasma cells which secrete antibodies against the antigen that activated them.

The antibodies can enter the bloodstream and travel throughout the body.

**c)** **Antibody Functions: **

Plasma cells only live for a few days but each one of them can produce two thousand antibodies every second resulting in an enormous quantity of circulating antibodies.

The antibodies produced by plasma cells have several functions.

Since each antibody has atleast two antigen binding sites.

Antibodies can bind to more than one microbial cell resulting in agglutination or clumping.

This agglutination helps remove the microorganisms from the body.

Antibodies can also neutralize toxins by resisting bacteria by binding to critical portions of the toxins or blocking proteins necessary for attachment to cells.

**Primary Immune Response: ** o The humoral immune system experiences periods of ramping up and gradual diminishing in response to the foreign invader. After the first exposure to an antigen there is a lag period when the exposed person’s serum does not contain any detectable antibodies. After several days the concentration of antibodies slowly rises followed by a gradual decline. This response is called a primary immune response. **Secondary Immune Response: ** o During the primary immune response, some activated B-cells

Antibodies also act as opsonins for phagocytosis and activate the compliment system. differentiate into long lived memory cells. If these cells become stimulated by the original antigen even years later, they rapidly produce antibodies secreting plasma cells. This faster stronger response is called a secondary immune response or a memory response.

### Types of Humoral Immunity:

It is of further of two types; a) Active Immunity b) Passive Immunity **a)** **Active Immunity: **

Immunity to disease based on previous exposure to antigen is called as active immunity.

It can be of further two types i.e. Natural or Artificial

Natural: When a person is exposed to an infection (antigen) and becomes ill and in most cases survives then this immunity, developed against that disease is called naturally induced immunity or auto immune response.

**b)** **Passive Immunity: **

Artificial: The use of vaccines, which stimulate the production of antibodies in the body and making person immune against the disease of infection

In contrast to active immunity in which case the antigens are introduced to stimulate the production of antibodies by artificial or natural method; antibodies are inserted in the form of antisera, to make a person immune against a disease is called as passive immunity.

It is also of further two types i.e. Natural and Artificial

Natural: It is the passive immunity produced naturally. **E.g.** As in the case of transfer of antibodies from mother to fetus across the placenta.

Artificial. It is the insertion of antibodies by artificially. **E.g.** As in the case of active infections of hepatitis, rabies, snake bite venom passive immunity is introduced artificially.

### ii. Cell-Mediated Immunity:

Cell-mediated immunity is a host defense used to fight intracellular pathogens and abnormal body cells such as cancer cells The protagonists of cell-mediated immunity are T-lymphocytes which can be divided into two groups: **a)** **Helper T-cells (T** **H ** **cells): ** o Helper T-cells secrete cytokines to activate other cells of the immune system. o **Helper T-Cells Activation: **

Each helper T-cell produces a unique T-cell receptor (TCR) which corresponds to a specific antigen.

Each Helper T-cell also displays a receptor called CD4 on its surface which recognizes the MHC molecule of antigen presenting cells.

When helper T-cell encounters an antigen presenting cell with that antigen displayed in on MHC molecule it signals the antigen presenting cell to release Interleukin-1 (IL-1).

o **Helper T-cells Proliferation: **

The IL-1 stimulates the helper T-cell to produce IL-2 as well as IL-2 receptor. The helper T-cell can then auto stimulate itself keeping it active as it proliferates.

Once a helper T-cell becomes activated it proliferates forming a clone of cells specific to the same antigen.

Some of the helper T-cells differentiate into long lived memory T-cells.

o **Helper T-cells functions: **

But most mature to produce cytokines to help with the immune response.

Helper T-cells differentiate into two sub-populations called TH1 cells and TH2 cells

TH1 cells produce cytokines that primarily activate the cell-mediated immunity such as macrophages, cytotoxic t-cells and natural killer cells.

TH2 cells produce cytokines that activates the b- cells involved in humoral immunity.

**b)** **Cytotoxic T-cells (T** **C** ** cells): ** o Cytotoxic T-cells attack infected cells directly. o **Cytotoxic T-Cells activation; **

Each cytotoxic T-cell produces a unique T-cell receptor which corresponds to a specific antigen.

Each cytotoxic T-cell also produces a receptor called CD8 which recognizes the MHC molecule of infected cells.

When a cytotoxic T-cell encounters an infected cell with the specific antigen bound in an MHC molecule, the cytotoxic T-cell produces receptors for IL-2 and other cytokines such as gamma interferon or IFN- gamma.

o **Cytotoxic T-Cells Proliferation: **

The cytotoxic T-cell can then be activated by nearby cytokine secreting active helper T-cells.

Once a cytotoxic T-cell becomes activated it proliferates forming a clone of cells specific to the same antigen.

o **Cytotoxic T-cells functions: **

Some of these cytotoxic T-cells differentiate into long lived memory T-cells. But most mature to attack and destroy the infected cells.

Cytotoxic T-cells can destroy infected or abnormal cells through multiple pathways.

The best understood of this pathway is the perforin granzyme cytotoxic pathway.

When the cytotoxic T-cells encounter an infected cell with the target antigen bound in an MHC molecule. The T-Cells secrete two types of protein molecules i.e. **perforin and granzyme.**

The perforin forms a pore in the membrane of the target cell allowing granzyme to enter the cell.

The granzyme activates the enzymes inside the cell leading to cell death.

## VACCINE

**Definition: ** “A vaccine is a biological preparation that improves immunity to a particular disease. A vaccine typically contains an agent that resembles a disease-causing microorganism .” **Types of Vaccines **

### 1. Whole Microorganisms Vaccine:

Many of the current vaccines in use for humans that are effective against viral and bacterial diseases consist of whole organisms that are either inactivated (killed) or attenuated (live but nonvirulent). These are termed whole organism’s vaccines. **Types: **

Attenuated whole microorganisms as vaccine

Inactivated whole microorganisms as vaccine

**i.** Attenuated Whole Microorganisms Vaccine: Such vaccines use living but attenuated (weakened) microbes. Life-long immunity, especially with virus is often achieved without booster immunization. Its effectiveness is about 95%. Long-term effectiveness probably occur because the attenuated virus replicate in the body, increasing the dose and acting as a series of secondary metabolites. **Examples** Measles, mumps and rubella injected S.C Sabin polio vaccine given Orally Chicken and small pox vaccines injected S.C Typhoid vaccine given Orally Tuberculosis bacillus injected ID

**ii.** Inactivated Whole Microorganisms Vaccine: Such vaccines use killed microbes. They are killed by treating with formalin or phenol. Its effectiveness and long-term immunity is compromised. **Examples ** Influenza vaccine injected S.C Rabies vaccine injected IM Salk polio vaccine injected IM Hepatitis A vaccine injected S.C Cholera vaccine injected S.C Pneumococcal pneumonia injected S.C Whooping cough vaccine Typhoid vaccine injected S.C

### 2. Purified Macromolecules as Vaccine:

Such vaccines use only specific, purified macromolecules derived from pathogenic microorganism. **Types **

**i.** Toxoids

**ii.** Subunit Vaccines

**iii.** Conjugated Vaccines

**i.** Toxoids: A substance that has been treated to destroy its toxic properties but retains the capacity to stimulate production of antitoxins. Such vaccines contain inactivated exotoxins. It requires a series of injection for full immunity followed by the booster every 10 years. Booster is for children not for adults. **Example** Tetanus (purified tetanus toxoids) injected IM Diphtheria (purified diphtheria toxoids) injected IM

**ii.** Subunit Vaccines: Such vaccines use those antigenic fragments of a microorganism that best stimulate an immune response. **Example** Pili of bacteria extracted and purified for use in vaccines to produce anti- pili antibodies. So, bacteria cannot attach to the tissues and phagocytized. Tuberculosis Vaccines Influenza Virus Vaccine

**iii.** Conjugated Vaccines: Such vaccines based on capsular polysaccharides and used for poor immune response children. Polysaccharides are T-independent antigens. So, children immune system does not respond well to these antigens until age of 15-24 months. In this vaccine polysaccharides are combined with proteins like diphtheria toxoids to produce conjugated vaccine. **Example** Hemophilus influenzae type b

### 3. Recombinant Vector Vaccines:

This form of vaccines applies the principle of isolating genes that encode major antigen from the pathogen and insert them into non-virulent viruses or bacteria. These vaccines are delivered by needle injected or by a device called a gene gun. The vector replicating within the host and expressing the gene product of the pathogen encoded antigenic proteins. These are experimental vaccines and work is going on measles, HIV.

### 4. DNA Vaccines:

A DNA vaccine elicits protective immunity against a microbial pathogen by activating both humoral and cellular immunity. Long lasting memory cells are also generated. These are also in trail and influenza and herpes has been tried on humans. **Gene Gun ** Gene guns have also been used to deliver DNA vaccines.

### 4. Radio Immunoassay:

A technique for determining antibody levels by introducing an antigen labelled with a radioisotope and measuring the subsequent radioactivity of the antibody component. **Method: ** o To perform a radioimmunoassay, a known quantity of an antigen is made radioactive, frequently by labeling it with gamma-radioactive isotopes of iodine, such as 125-I, attached to tyrosine. o This radiolabeled antigen is then mixed with a known amount of antibody for that antigen, and as a result, the two specifically bind to one another. o Then, a sample of serum from a patient containing an unknown quantity of that same antigen is added. This causes the unlabeled (or "cold") antigen from the serum to compete with the radiolabeled antigen ("hot") for antibody binding sites. o As the concentration of "cold" antigen is increased, more of it binds to the antibody, displacing the radiolabeled variant, and reducing the ratio of antibody- bound radiolabeled antigen to free radiolabeled antigen. o The bound antigens are then separated from the unbound ones, and the radioactivity of the free(unbound) antigen remaining in the [supernatant] is measured using a gamma counter.

### 5. Agglutination Tests:
